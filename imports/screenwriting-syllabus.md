**Loop regions:** Modules 4–7 are the loop. You will run them once per draft, forever. Everything else you learn once.

## EVIDENCE STATUS KEY

The single most useful thing in this syllabus. Screenwriting craft is ~85% inherited intuition and ~15% tested. Knowing which is which stops you defending the wrong thing in a room.

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[E1]** | Empirically supported — replicated experimental or corpus evidence |
| **[E2]** | Partially supported — real mechanism, contested detail or thin replication |
| **[E3]** | Descriptive scholarship — rigorous textual/historical analysis, not experimental |
| **[E4]** | Craft heuristic — no empirical basis. Often *works*. Not evidence |
| **[E5]** | Folklore — widely repeated, actively disputed or disproven |

Use these when you teach. "This is an E4 — it's how the room thinks, not a law" is a sentence that earns you enormous credibility.

# TRACK F — THE REFERENCE TRACK
## Module 0: How an audience actually processes a film

Do this **first**, not last. Every craft rule downstream is either an application of these findings or an unfalsified guess, and you can only tell the difference if you know this material.

### 0.1 Event segmentation

Audiences don't perceive continuous flow. They chunk experience into **events**, and segment at points of prediction failure — when the model of "what happens next" breaks.

- Zacks, Speer, Swallow, Braver & Reynolds (2007), *Event perception: A mind-brain perspective*, **Psychological Bulletin** 133(2) **[E1]**
- Magliano & Zacks (2011), *The impact of continuity editing in narrative film on event segmentation*, **Cognitive Science** 35(8) **[E1]**

**What it gives you as a writer:** a scene break is not a formatting convention. It's a cognitive event boundary. This is the empirical grounding for "enter late, leave early" — you are placing the boundary deliberately rather than letting the audience place it for you.

### 0.2 Attentional synchrony and edit blindness

Under continuity editing, viewers look at nearly the same place at nearly the same time. Cuts placed on that convergence are literally not seen.

- Smith, T.J. (2012), *The Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity*, **Projections** 6(1) **[E1]**
- Smith & Henderson (2008), *Edit blindness*, **Journal of Eye Movement Research** 2(2) **[E1]**

**For you:** this is the real mechanism under your front-load model. Motion and faces capture gaze pre-attentively and involuntarily. Continuity editing works because it *exploits* attention, not because of an aesthetic convention.

### 0.3 Shot length structure

Corpus analysis of 150 Hollywood films found shot-length sequences increasingly approximating a **1/f pattern** — the fractal structure found in music, natural sound, and endogenous attention rhythms.

- Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer (2010), *Attention and the evolution of Hollywood film*, **Psychological Science** 21(3) **[E1]**
- Cutting, Brunick, DeLong, Iricinschi & Candan (2011), *Quicker, faster, darker: Changes in Hollywood film over 75 years*, **i-Perception** 2(6) **[E1]**
- Cutting (2016), *Narrative theory and the dynamics of popular movies*, **Psychonomic Bulletin & Review** 23(6) **[E1]**

**For you:** this is the strongest empirical support your duration formula has. It also tells you the variance rule matters more than the mean.

### 0.4 Transportation and narrative engagement

Absorbed audiences reduce counter-arguing and process affectively. Absorption is a **state**, not a stimulus — it cannot be forced.

- Green & Brock (2000), *The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives*, **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology** 79(5) **[E1]**
- Busselle & Bilandzic (2009), *Measuring narrative engagement*, **Media Psychology** 12(4) **[E1]**
- Slater & Rouner (2002), *Entertainment-education and elaboration likelihood*, **Communication Theory** 12(2) **[E2]**

**For you:** direct empirical backing for your claim that acceptance requires relationship and cannot be forced. This is the paper to cite in a client deck.

### 0.5 Suspense and attentional narrowing

Suspense measurably narrows attentional focus — peripheral processing drops during high-suspense passages.

- Bezdek, Gerrig et al. (2015), *Neural evidence that suspense narrows attentional focus*, **Neuroscience** 303 **[E2]**
- Vorderer, Wulff & Friedrichsen, eds. (1996), *Suspense: Conceptualizations, Theoretical Analyses, and Empirical Explorations* **[E2]**

**For you:** the empirical version of your Gold Rule. During peak Load, the audience literally stops taking in the world around the point of focus. Which is exactly why they cannot encode anything until you release.

### 0.6 Identification and disposition

Audiences form moral judgements of characters and their enjoyment is a function of outcomes matching those judgements.

- Zillmann (1996), *Affective disposition theory* **[E1]**
- Raney (2004), *Expanding disposition theory*, **Communication Theory** 14(4) **[E2]**
- Cohen (2001), *Defining identification*, **Mass Communication and Society** 4(3) **[E2]**
- Oliver & Bartsch (2010), *Appreciation as audience response*, **Human Communication Research** 36(1) **[E2]** — the eudaimonic/"meaningful" axis, distinct from enjoyment

**For you:** this is why "make the hero likeable" is bad advice and "make the audience *want them to get what's coming*" is good advice. Disposition, not likeability.

### 0.7 The cautionary module — where film lore fails

**The Kuleshov effect.** Universally taught. The best-controlled modern attempt did **not** find the classic result.

- Barratt, Rédei, Innes-Ker & van de Weijer (2016), *Does the Kuleshov effect really exist?*, **Perception** 45(8) **[E5]**
- Calbi et al. (2017), **Frontiers in Psychology** — partial, weaker effects **[E2]**

Juxtaposition *does* create meaning. But the specific claim that a neutral face reads as hunger/grief/desire depending on the adjacent shot is not robustly supported. **Teach the principle, drop the anecdote.**

# TRACK A — STORY
## Module 1: Premise and controlling idea

### Craft canon
- McKee, *Story* (1997) — controlling idea, value charge **[E4]**
- Aristotle, *Poetics* — the source of nearly everything **[E3]**
- Yorke, *Into the Woods* (2013) — five-act argument **[E4]**

### Scholarly
- Macdonald, I.W. (2013), *Screenwriting Poetics and the Screen Idea* **[E3]** — the concept of the "screen idea" as a collectively held object, not a document. The best academic account of what a premise *is* institutionally.
- **Journal of Screenwriting** (Intellect, est. 2010) — the field's only dedicated peer-reviewed venue. Read it if you want to teach this credibly.

### Formula
```
LOGLINE:  When [INCITING INCIDENT], a [FLAWED PROTAGONIST]
          must [GOAL] or [STAKES] — but [OBSTACLE].

CONTROLLING IDEA:  [VALUE] triumphs/is destroyed because [CAUSE].
```

### Exercise
Write 20 loglines in one sitting. Keep 2. The discard rate is the lesson.

# TRACK B — ARCHITECTURE
## Module 2: Structure

### Craft canon
- Field, *Screenplay* (1979) — the paradigm, plot points **[E4]**
- Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005) — the 15 story beats **[E4]**
- Gulino, *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (2004) — 8 sequences **[E4]**
- Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007) — 22 steps **[E4]**

### Scholarly / descriptive
- Thompson, K. (1999), *Storytelling in the New Hollywood* **[E3]** — the four-part structure argument, derived from actual textual analysis rather than assertion. **This is the honest alternative to Field.**
- Bordwell, Staiger & Thompson (1985), *The Classical Hollywood Cinema* **[E3]**
- Bordwell (2006), *The Way Hollywood Tells It* **[E3]**
- Cutting (2016) **[E1]** — found statistical support for act-like structure in shot-length and scene-length distributions across a large corpus. **The only quantitative evidence that three/four-act structure is real rather than imposed.**

### The honest position
Three-act structure is **[E4] as prescription, [E3]–[E1] as description.** Films do tend to display these shapes. That does not mean the shape caused the film to work. Teach it as a default the industry reads scripts against — which is a real professional fact — not a law of narrative.

### The terminology fix
```
STORY BEAT   (Snyder)  = structural landmark, 5–15 pages, ~15 per film
MICRO-BEAT   (McKee)   = action/reaction exchange, 5–30 sec, 400–800 per film
```
Two different words in the industry, one syllable. Name them separately in every document you write.

# TRACK C — PEOPLE
## Module 3: Character

### Craft canon
- Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946) — premise, orchestration of character **[E4]**
- Truby (2007) — moral argument, weakness/need **[E4]**
- Vogler, *The Writer's Journey* (1992) — archetypes **[E4]**, derived from Campbell **[E5] as anthropology, [E4] as toolkit**

### Scholarly
- Mar & Oatley (2008), *The function of fiction is the abstraction and simulation of social experience*, **Perspectives on Psychological Science** 3(3) **[E2]** — narrative as social simulation
- Smith, M. (1995), *Engaging Characters* **[E3]** — recognition/alignment/allegiance, the best analytic model of how audiences attach
- Cohen (2001) **[E2]**

### Formula
```
WANT   — external, conscious, drives plot
NEED   — internal, unconscious, drives theme
WOUND  — the past event
LIE    — the false belief the wound installed
ARC    — the moment they choose NEED over WANT

ANTAGONIST — same want, incompatible method, right from own POV
```

### Note on the monomyth
Campbell's Hero's Journey is **[E5] as a claim about universal human myth** — comparative-mythology scholarship has largely rejected the universality thesis. It remains **[E4]** and genuinely useful as a story-generation tool. Say the second thing, not the first.

# TRACK D — CRAFT (LOOP REGION ▶◀)
## Module 4: The scene

### Craft canon
- McKee (1997) — value charge, the turn **[E4]**
- Mamet, *On Directing Film* (1991) — the most ruthless show-don't-tell argument **[E4]**
- Bickham, *Scene & Structure* (1993) — scene/sequel **[E4]**

### Evidence link
Module 0.1 (event segmentation) **[E1]**. Scene boundaries are cognitive boundaries. Late-in/early-out has a mechanism.

### The seven-question scene card
```
1. Whose scene is it?              (one POV owner)
2. What do they want IN THIS ROOM? (scene objective ≠ film objective)
3. Why now?                        (if it could be tomorrow, cut it)
4. What's in the way?              (another want. never weather)
5. What tactics?                   (charm → guilt → threat → withdraw)
6. Where is the turn?              (control changes hands)
7. Charge in → charge out          (+→− or −→+. NEVER same-to-same)
```

### The deletion test
Remove each scene. Does the next one still make sense? If yes, it was decoration. **First drafts are ~40% removable.**

## Module 5: Micro-beat and subtext

### Craft canon
- Stanislavski, *An Actor Prepares* — the origin of "beat" (bit/unit of action) **[E4]**
- McKee (1997) **[E4]**
- Seger, *Writing Subtext* (2011) **[E4]**

### Evidence link
Module 0.5 **[E2]** — suspense narrows attention. Beat-level Load/Absorb alternation is what keeps a scene from flattening under sustained load.

### Formula
```
MICRO-BEAT = ACTION (tactic) → REACTION (resistance) → ADJUSTMENT (new tactic)

Scene = 3–8 micro-beats.
1 beat  = an announcement, not a scene
20 beats = two scenes wearing one slugline
```

**The turn is always the longest Absorb beat in the scene.** Not the loudest. The longest.

## Module 6: Image and interiority conversion

**The central skill. If you teach one module, teach this.**

### Craft canon
- Mamet (1991) **[E4]**
- Seger, *The Art of Adaptation* (1992) **[E4]**

### Scholarly
- Hutcheon (2006), *A Theory of Adaptation* **[E3]** — telling vs. showing vs. interacting modes; the definitive academic treatment
- Stam (2000), *Beyond Fidelity: The Dialogics of Adaptation* **[E3]**
- Andrew (1984), *Concepts in Film Theory* **[E3]** — borrowing / intersecting / transforming taxonomy

### The conversion formula
```
[INTERNAL STATE] → [OBSERVABLE BEHAVIOUR + OBJECT]
```

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| He's ashamed | Takes the long route past the shop |
| She's decided to leave | Buys one ticket |
| He's losing control | The bucket under the drip stops being emptied |
| They've lost money | Twelve chairs, two plates |

### The camera test
> *Can a camera photograph this, or a mic record it?*

Words that fail it: **remembers, knows, realizes, symbolizes, seems like the kind of man who, feels, has always been.**

### The object rule
Every scene has **one physical thing the conflict travels through.** The object carries the plot so the dialogue doesn't have to.

## Module 7: Environment, ensemble, register

### Craft canon
- Mascelli, *The Five C's of Cinematography* (1965) **[E4]** — camera angles, continuity, cutting, close-ups, composition
- Katz, *Film Directing Shot by Shot* (1991) **[E4]**

### Evidence link
- Module 0.2 **[E1]** — attentional synchrony: background life only reads if it doesn't compete for gaze at the moment of the turn
- Habituation to sustained stimulus **[E1]** — the empirical basis for comic relief and register rhythm

### The five layers
```
1. FOREGROUND  — the conflict                    → plot
2. OBJECT      — what conflict travels through   → makes it physical
3. ENVIRONMENT — light source, sound, decay      → makes it a place
4. BACKGROUND  — NPCs with their own business    → makes it a world
5. REGISTER    — who's allowed to be funny here  → makes it bearable
```
**Every scene carries foreground + at least two others.** All five is usually clutter.

### Writing light (not lighting)
| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| "Low-key, hard shadows" | "One tube light. Half the room stays dark." |
| "Warm golden glow" | "Late sun through the grille. Bars across the floor." |

**Rule: light must be motivated by an object in the room or a decision by a person.**

### The indifference rule
> The world does not care that your protagonist is having the most important day of his life.

Background characters pursue their own objectives. The moment they all react to the hero, the world becomes a stage.

### The ensemble register chart
| Register | Function |
|---|---|
| **The Weight** | Carries theme. Usually humourless |
| **The Voice** | Says what the hero won't admit — externalised interiority |
| **The Release** | Comic. Makes the film survivable |
| **The Mirror** | Same wound, opposite choice |
| **The Anchor** | Represents what's being fought for |
| **The Disruptor** | Breaks stasis. Amoral |

### The four comic-character laws
1. Must be **competent** at something real
2. Must **not know** he is funny
3. Must have **one scene where he is not funny** — this converts device to person
4. Comedy comes **after** tension, never during

**The withdrawal is the technique.** Remove the Release in Act IIb. The audience feels the temperature drop without knowing why.

# TRACK E — LANGUAGE
## Module 8: Dialogue

### Craft canon
- McKee, *Dialogue* (2016) **[E4]**
- Mamet (1991) **[E4]**
- Sorkin / Chayefsky / Salim–Javed — read produced scripts, not books

### Scholarly
- Kozloff (2000), *Overhearing Film Dialogue* **[E3]** — the only serious academic study of film dialogue's functions. Essential and almost unknown.
- Bednarek (2010), *The Language of Fictional Television* **[E3]** — corpus-linguistic analysis of scripted speech vs. natural speech **[E1]** on the descriptive claims

### Formula
```
TEXT    = what is said
SUBTEXT = what is wanted
GOOD DIALOGUE: TEXT ≠ SUBTEXT
```

### The four cuts
1. Cut any line stating an emotion the actor can play
2. Cut any line delivering information both speakers already know
3. Cut any line that answers the previous line directly (people deflect)
4. Cut any line replaceable by a look, an object, or a silence

**The name test:** cover the character names. If you can't tell who's speaking, you have one character in several costumes.

### Regional note
In Indian film industries, **screenplay and dialogue are separately credited crafts.** A dialogue writer builds idiom, star register, and elevation moments on top of another writer's structure. Hollywood does the same work and calls it a "dialogue polish," usually uncredited due to WGA arbitration rules. Know both vocabularies.

# TRACK A (RETURN) — POLISH
## Module 9: Adaptation

### Canon
- Hutcheon (2006) **[E3]** — start here, not with Seger
- Stam (2000) **[E3]**
- Andrew (1984) **[E3]**
- Leitch (2007), *Film Adaptation and Its Discontents* **[E3]**
- Seger (1992) **[E4]** — practical, weakest theoretically

### The four operations
```
1. COMPRESS    — three characters become one composite
2. CUT         — whole subplots go
3. EXTERNALISE — thought becomes behaviour + object   ← Module 6
4. INVENT      — write scenes the book only summarised
```

### The fidelity trap
Contemporary adaptation studies has largely abandoned fidelity as an evaluative criterion **[E3]**. A film is not a failed copy of a novel; it's a different work in dialogue with it. *Blade Runner* keeps a mood and a question and almost nothing else. This is not a defect.

## Module 10: Format, rewriting, and the industry as it is

### Format
- Courier 12pt, 1.5" left margin, monospaced
- **1 page ≈ 1 minute** — a property of the *format*, not of storytelling **[E4]**, reliable in aggregate, unreliable per scene
- Feature 90–120pp. TV hour 55–65pp. Multi-cam sitcom 45–55pp (double-spaced dialogue — rule doesn't apply)

### Scholarly
- Maras (2009), *Screenwriting: History, Theory and Practice* **[E3]** — the best account of *why* the format is what it is, and of the script's contested status as text vs. blueprint
- Price (2013), *A History of the Screenplay* **[E3]**
- Nelmes, ed. (2011), *Analysing the Screenplay* **[E3]**

### Rewriting — separate passes, in order
```
1. STRUCTURE  — does the spine hold?
2. CHARACTER  — one character at a time, top to bottom
3. DIALOGUE   — read aloud, cut 30%
4. IMAGE      — camera-test every action line
5. POLISH     — format, typos, page count
```

### The industry reality module
Teach this or your students will be blindsided:
- 3–8 writers on a produced feature is **normal**, not failure
- Work-for-hire: you usually do not own it
- The director restructures. The DoP rebreaks the schedule. The budget rewrites Act III. The edit is the final rewrite
- **You cannot fix in the edit what was never on the page** — but a good script can still be ruined by execution. The asymmetry is real

# THE LOOP
## Module 11: The scene-build sequence

Run this every time. This is the loop region.

```
1.  Write the scene logline
2.  Set the charge          (+ → −  or  − → +)
3.  Choose the object
4.  Choose the location     (and its one light source)
5.  Find the turn
6.  Write it too long       (deliberately)
7.  Delete first + last speech
8.  Apply the four dialogue cuts
9.  Camera-test every action line
10. Map micro-beats: Load / Absorb alternation
11. Deletion test — if nothing breaks, cut the scene
        ↺ back to 1
```

## READING ORDER (if you only do six things)

1. **Mamet, *On Directing Film*** — 100 pages. Read in one sitting. It will make you delete half your script.
2. **McKee, *Story*** — the scene turn and value charge. Skim the rest.
3. **Kozloff, *Overhearing Film Dialogue*** — the one genuinely rigorous book on dialogue.
4. **Cutting (2016), *Narrative theory and the dynamics of popular movies*** — the single best paper linking structure to measurable audience response.
5. **Thompson, *Storytelling in the New Hollywood*** — structure argued from evidence rather than assertion.
6. **Maras, *Screenwriting: History, Theory and Practice*** — why the blueprint is a blueprint.

Then read twenty produced screenplays. That's worth more than the six books.

## A NOTE ON THE EVIDENCE

Most screenwriting instruction has never been tested and probably cannot be — you can't randomise audiences to structural conditions and hold everything else constant. That's not a scandal; it's a property of the object.

What *has* been tested is the **audience side**: attention, segmentation, transportation, disposition, suspense. That literature is real, replicated, and almost entirely absent from screenwriting education.

So the honest framing for this syllabus, and the one worth teaching:

> **The craft rules are hypotheses. The audience research is the closest thing we have to a test bench. Where a craft rule has a mechanism in Track F, it's load-bearing. Where it doesn't, it's a convention — often a good one, but hold it loosely.**

Verify citation details before publishing or teaching from this; I've given author/year/venue so they're traceable, but check them.
